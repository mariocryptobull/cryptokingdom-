# Treadmill: facts and sources

| Claim in script / on screen | Source(s) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Speed sensor = magnet on the front roller pulley + reed switch (or Hall sensor); one closure per revolution, console counts pulses | Precor 9.23 service manual, "Procedure 5.3 – Troubleshooting the Speed Sensor" (manualslib.com/manual/1166165, p. 21: open circuit away from magnet, ~0 Ω aligned); ProForm 775 CT service manual (manualsonline, p. 22: magnet-to-reed-switch gap ~1/8 in); Empire Fitness Services "Speed Sensors" | Some models use two magnets per pulley or a Hall-effect / optical sensor; "reed or Hall" on screen covers both. |
| Each pulse = one roller circumference of belt; speed = rpm × circumference | US Patent 5,368,532 "Treadmill having an automatic speed control system" (front roller circumference used to compute belt travel); Arduino forum "Custom Treadmill Display" (2.5 in roller → ~7.85 in per pulse) | Plain geometry: v = N × π × D. |
| Worked example: π × 2.5 in = 7.85 in; × 700 rpm = 5,495 in/min ≈ 5.2 mph | Computed: 7.854 in × 700 = 5,498 in/min = 458 ft/min = 5.21 mph | Spoken as "about five miles an hour". 2.5 in is a common front-roller diameter (forum example above); 700 rpm chosen for the example. |
| DC motor + motor control board (PWM) drive the belt; flywheel on the motor | allaboutcircuits / hobby-machinist forum threads on treadmill PM DC motors and control boards; KB Electronics KBMM-125 treadmill controller note (industrialmonitordirect.com); treadmill motor duty rating tied to flywheel (forum) | Commercial machines often use AC motors with a VFD; script says "a motor and its controller". |
| Foot strike brakes the belt; controller raises current to restore speed | US Patent 5,476,430 "Exercise treadmill with variable response to foot impact induced speed variation"; Savelberg et al. 1998, Gait & Posture 7:26–34 "Intra-stride belt-speed variation affects treadmill locomotion" (variation depends on treadmill power and subject mass) | The scope graphic is schematic (shape, not measured data). |
| Belt still dips about 4–9 % per step | Radstake 2000, MSc thesis, McMaster University, "Speed variation and power transfer during treadmill running": intra-step belt-speed variation 4.2–8.6 % of mean belt speed (5 runners, 55–100 kg, 2.6–4.0 m/s); Willwacher et al. 2021, Sci Rep (PMC7838258): larger dips with higher body mass | Spoken as "four to nine percent". |
| Lubricated deck; deck cushions soften landings | Treadmill owner/service manuals (silicone deck lubrication, elastomer cushions) — general construction | Not quantified in VO. |
| Incline motor turns a lead screw to tilt the deck | Hobbyist wiring descriptions (separate incline motor switched up/down by the power board; linear actuator / screw) | On screen "0 → 10 %": many home treadmills offer 10–15 % max; shown as an example. |
| Safety key: magnet clip on a cord; pulling it stops the belt | Wahoo KICKR RUN "Safety Features" support page; Sunny Health & Fitness "Treadmills: Safety Keys"; Precor 9.23 manual "Treadmill Safety Features" | |
| Treadmill feels easier: no air resistance | Jones & Doust 1996 (premise: "the lack of air resistance results in a lower energy cost compared with running outdoors at the same velocity") | "Belt pulls your feet back for you" is the common lay explanation; on a constant-speed belt the mechanics are close to overground running, and air resistance is the main measured difference. |
| 1 % incline matches outdoor running effort | Jones AM, Doust JH (1996) "A 1% treadmill grade most accurately reflects the energetic cost of outdoor running", J Sports Sci 14(4):321–327, doi:10.1080/02640419608727717 | 9 trained runners, 2.92–5.0 m/s (≈ 6.5–11 mph). At the slowest speeds, 0 % and 1 % both matched road running. |
